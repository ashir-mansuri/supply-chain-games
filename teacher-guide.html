Teacher’s guide

Three team games and six solo games. Each team game is built to fill one 60-minute class: about half the time playing and the rest discussing what happened. Students learn more from the debrief than from the game itself, so protect that time.

Running a session

Solo games. Boundary Bakes, Season Planner, Export Desk, Go Global, Factory Footprint and Freight Buyer are played alone, in class or as homework. There is no class code or login: students type their name and the game code you give them, play, and use Copy my result on the final report to send you a one-line result. Everyone with the same game code faces the same demand and the same events, so results can be compared.

Each team game can be played in two ways:

  • Online team play. Every student uses their own phone or laptop. Team members log in with the same team name and PIN, and their screens stay in sync. In Supplier Showdown each member manages some of the four parts, and orders go out only when every member taps Ready. In Warehouse Rush members take turns as the picker while the others help plan the route. In Coffee Chain each member runs one stage of the supply chain and sees only that stage. Your teacher dashboard shows every team’s progress and score live. This works in a classroom and in an online class on Google Meet or Zoom (put teams in breakout rooms so they can talk). It needs the one-time setup below.
  • Practice on one device. A team gathers round one phone or laptop. No setup or class code is needed, and it works even if online play isn’t switched on. You type each team’s result into the practice scoreboard on your projector.
  • Devices. Any phone, tablet or laptop with an up-to-date browser (Chrome, Safari, Edge or Firefox). Nothing to install. Laptops and tablets show the warehouse map larger.
  • Teams. 3 to 5 students for Supplier Showdown (it rewards discussion); 2 to 4 for Warehouse Rush; exactly 4 for Coffee Chain if you can (one per stage).
  • Same events for everyone. All teams in one online class face the same news (Supplier Showdown) or the same warehouse and orders (Warehouse Rush), so results compare fairly. For practice games, write one game code on the board instead.
  • Don’t use private or incognito windows. The game remembers a student’s team in the browser, so they can rejoin after a refresh.
  • Privacy. Online, the only personal detail stored is the first name a student types, in your own Firebase project, until you delete the class. Practice games store nothing outside the device.

All companies, products, prices and events are made up for teaching. They are realistic in direction (Chinese parts are cheaper but further away and riskier; local parts cost more but arrive faster) but not real market data.

Online team play

Online play needs a small free database so that phones can share a game. The site uses Google Firebase, which is free for a class of this size: up to 100 devices connected at once (enough for about 90 students plus your dashboard), with no payment card. You set it up once, in about 15 minutes, and it then works for every class.

Running an online class

  1. Before class: open the teacher dashboard, choose Create a class, pick the game and the number of teams, and set a teacher password. You get a class code and a 4-digit PIN for each team. Rename teams if you like.
  2. Start of class: put the class code on the board or in the meeting chat, with the game’s web address. Give each team only its own PIN, for example on a slip of paper or by message.
  3. Students join: each student opens the game, chooses Join your team, enters the class code, picks their team, types the PIN and their first name.
  4. During play: keep the dashboard open. It shows who is online in each team, where each team has got to, and live scores. Projector view shows just the class code and the leaderboard.
  5. If a team gets stuck: Restart game on the dashboard starts that team again from the beginning. If a student drops out, their parts or picking turns pass to teammates automatically.
  6. After class: discuss the results on the projector, then Delete this class to remove the students’ names and results.

Supplier Showdown

Teams run purchasing for Sabar Scooters, an electric scooter maker in Sanand. Each scooter needs four parts: a battery pack, a hub motor, a controller chip and a tyre set. Each can come from four suppliers: Shenzhen (China), Hanoi (Vietnam), Pune (India) and Stuttgart (Germany). The game runs from April to November. Every month teams split their orders between suppliers and choose a buffer stock level, and then a news card is drawn. The previous manager sourced everything from Shenzhen because it was cheapest, so every team starts there.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the class, students should be able to:

  1. Tell apart quoted price, landed cost and total cost of ownership, and calculate each one.
  2. Compare single sourcing with dual or multiple sourcing in terms of cost and risk.
  3. Explain what buffer (safety) stock does and what it costs.
  4. Explain why the scarcest component limits output when a product needs several parts.
  5. Argue a position on the trade-off between efficiency and resilience, using evidence from the game.

60-minute lesson plan

0–8Hook and rules. Ask: “If one supplier is 25% cheaper, why would anyone buy from the other?” Take a quick show of hands. Project the start screen and go through the four rules in the box. Write the game code on the board.
8–10Teams start. Online: students join their team with the class code and PIN. Practice: each team opens the game on one device and types the game code.
10–40Play eight months, about 3 to 4 minutes per month. Keep the class roughly together: call “two minutes to place orders”. Walk around and ask teams to explain their split. Don’t give away what is coming.
40–52Results and debrief. Show the leaderboard (online, it is already on your dashboard; for practice games, type each team’s result into the practice scoreboard). Ask two contrasting teams to explain their strategy. Then work through the discussion questions on the final report (answers below).
52–60Theory wrap-up. Put the total-cost formula on the board, connect dual sourcing and buffer stock to the risk the class just lived through, and set the follow-up task.

Short on time? Give about 2 minutes per month after the first two. Teams speed up once they know the screens. The final report, with its comparison against three fixed strategies, appears after all eight months.

How the game works

Demand. The planning screen shows the dealers’ forecast (950 in April, rising to 1,400 in October’s festive season). Actual orders land within 6% of the forecast, plus any news effect.

Online teamwork. The four parts are shared out among the team members who are online (with two members, each manages two parts). A member can change only their own parts; anyone can change the buffer stock, which asks everyone to confirm again. The month is played when every member online has tapped Ready.

Ordering. Teams don’t type quantities. The game orders forecast + buffer target − stock in hand, for each part, and splits that order by the percentages the team chose. This keeps the focus on the sourcing decision. Orders arrive within the month; lead time shows up as the cost of money tied up in transit and as how long a disruption lasts.

Production. Scooters built = the smallest of (dealer orders, and stock plus good arrivals for each of the four parts). One missing part stops the line, however much of the others is in the store.

New suppliers. Approving a supplier for a part costs ₹5 lakh once, and it ships only half its share in its first month. Switching after a crisis is slow and costly; diversifying before one is cheaper.

Capacity. Pune can supply at most 60% of battery packs and 40% of chips, so a fully local strategy isn’t possible.

Reliability and quality. Each month a supplier may ship slightly short (on average about 7% for Shenzhen, 5% Hanoi, 3% Pune, 1% Stuttgart). Faulty parts, at around each supplier’s defect rate, are paid for and scrapped.

Money.

Selling price to dealers₹75,000 per scooter
Assembly and other variable cost₹12,000 per scooter built
Fixed costs₹1.5 crore per month
Dealer penalty₹5,000 per scooter not delivered (plus the lost sale)
Storing stock2% of part price per month
Money tied up in transit1.5% of part price per month of lead time
Approving a new supplier₹5 lakh per supplier per part, once

Monthly profit counts the parts actually used (at average cost), not the cash spent on restocking, so building a buffer doesn’t show up as a loss in the month you buy it.

Every game has the same pattern: a quiet April, a major China disruption in June or July, a Pune disruption in August or September (with a warning on the planning screen that month), one global shock, one quality problem and the October festive rush. Debrief: answer points

1. Which month hurt you most? What could you have done before it happened?
Usually the China disruption month. Teams that had already approved a second supplier and held some buffer lost far less. Reacting after the event is too late: new suppliers ship only half in month one and cost ₹5 lakh each. Point: resilience must be bought in advance, like insurance.
2. Compare quoted price with landed cost. What else did cheap parts cost you?
Duty and freight shrink the price gap; transit time ties up money; higher defect rates mean paying for scrap; and the long, risky supply line caused lost sales and dealer penalties. This is total cost of ownership: the cheapest quote is rarely the cheapest supplier.
3. A new supplier ships only half in its first month. What does that mean for switching after a crisis?
Qualifying a supplier takes time (audits, samples, ramp-up). In a crisis every buyer is switching at once, so capacity is scarce too. Keep a second supplier active, even at a small share, so it can be scaled up.
4. When was buffer stock worth what it cost?
When it covered a short disruption or the festive demand spike. It was wasted when nothing went wrong, and it can’t cover a long outage like a two-month fire. Buffer stock handles short, uncertain gaps; dual sourcing handles long ones.
5. Which part was your weakest link?
Often the battery pack (biggest cost, Pune capacity cap) or the chip (shortage event, Pune capped at 40%). In assembly, output is set by the scarcest part, so risk must be managed part by part, not just for the most expensive one.
6. What would you tell the board about cheap versus safe?
A good answer weighs both: a modest cost premium for a second source and a small buffer protects revenue worth far more. Strong answers use numbers from their own game, such as profit lost in the worst month compared with the cost of diversifying.

Follow-up work

  1. Total cost worksheet. Using the supplier table above, calculate landed cost and true cost per good part for each battery supplier. Which supplier wins on quoted price, and which on true cost?
  2. Weighted supplier scorecard. Score the four motor suppliers out of 10 on cost, quality, delivery and risk. Weight them 40/20/20/20, then 25/25/25/25. Does the winner change?
  3. Memo to the board. One page: recommend a sourcing strategy for next year, with evidence from the game and one real-world example of a supply disruption.

Warehouse Rush

Teams are pickers at a fulfilment centre near Ahmedabad during the festive sale. The warehouse has four aisles (A to D), each with eight bays on both sides: 64 slots holding 36 products. Teams pick the same six customer orders three times. In round 1 the layout is random. In round 2 they first study last month’s sales and re-slot the warehouse. In round 3 they batch-pick two orders per trip. After every trip the game shows the shortest possible route for the same shelves.

Learning outcomes

  1. Carry out an ABC analysis on sales data and explain the Pareto pattern.
  2. Explain how slotting fast movers near dispatch cuts travel time.
  3. Compare single-order picking with batch picking, including the trade-offs.
  4. Measure picking productivity in lines per hour and break picker time into its parts.

60-minute lesson plan

0–8Hook. Ask: “What does a picker spend most of their shift doing?” (Usually walking.) Show the start page and how picker time is counted. Write the code on the board.
8–18Round 1: Day one. Six orders on a random layout. Let teams notice how far apart the shelves are. Online, team members take turns as the picker, one trip each, while the others call out the route.
18–28ABC analysis. Everyone pauses at the sales chart. Ask: what share of picks do the top 7 products make? (67%.) Teams then re-slot by tapping shelves. “Do it for me” shows the ideal layout if a team is stuck.
28–36Round 2: ABC slotting. The same six orders on the new layout.
36–44Round 3: Batch picking. Three trips of two orders each.
44–56Debrief. Leaderboard first (round 1 and round 3 lines per hour; online it is on your dashboard), then the discussion questions on the results screen.
56–60Wrap-up. Connect to real warehouse practice and set the follow-up task.

How it works

  • Picker time per trip = 20 seconds setup + 10 seconds per line + walking distance ÷ 1.2 metres per second.
  • Lines per hour = lines picked ÷ total picker time × 3,600.
  • Walking follows the aisles: up and down an aisle, and between aisles only along the front or back cross-aisle. Every trip starts and ends at dispatch.
  • Shortest route is calculated exactly for each trip, so route efficiency (shortest ÷ yours) is a fair measure of route planning.
  • ABC classes: A = top 7 products (20% of products, 67% of picks); B = next 11 (19% of picks); C = last 18 (14% of picks).
  • Fast-pick zone: the 8 slots nearest dispatch, outlined in gold on the re-slotting map.
  • Walk per pick on the re-slotting screen is the average round trip from dispatch to a product, weighted by how often it sells. It drops from about 38 m on a random layout to 20 m on the best layout.

With perfect routes, the same orders run at about 112 lines per hour in round 1, 143 in round 2 and 186 in round 3 (average across test games). Students’ round 1 is usually lower, because routes are harder to plan when shelves are scattered.

Debrief: answer points

1. How much of the gain came from slotting, and how much from batch picking?
Compare rounds 1–2 (slotting only) with rounds 2–3 (batching only). Slotting cuts metres per line; batching cuts trips, so it saves setup time and walks back to dispatch.
2. Why does the Pareto pattern make ABC slotting so powerful?
Because a few products make most of the picks. Moving 7 products close to dispatch affects two-thirds of all picks, while moving the 18 C products barely matters.
3. Was your route close to the shortest one? When was it hardest to plan?
Hardest with many stops spread over several aisles, which is why real warehouses use routing rules (such as the S-shape route) or software. A good layout also makes routes easier, not just shorter.
4. When would batch picking not be worth it?
When orders must be sorted afterwards (extra time and errors), when orders are urgent and can’t wait to be grouped, or when orders are large enough to fill a trolley alone.
5. How often should a warehouse re-slot?
Demand changes with seasons and promotions (festive gifting, monsoon goods). Re-slotting costs labour and can disrupt picking, so warehouses re-slot the few products whose class has changed, often seasonally.
6. Where else could you save time?
Setup and pick time per line (scanners, pick-to-light), zone picking, better racking heights (the “golden zone” at waist height) and the building layout itself, such as dispatch placement.

Follow-up work

  1. ABC in a spreadsheet. Give students a list of 50 products with monthly sales. They sort, calculate the running share and draw the Pareto chart, then propose A, B and C cut-offs.
  2. Productivity sum. A picker does 140 lines an hour on an 8-hour shift with 1 hour of breaks. How many pickers are needed for 6,000 lines a day? What if batch picking raises the rate to 185?
  3. Site visit or video reflection. Watch a warehouse tour video and identify the picking method, the slotting logic and one improvement.

Coffee Chain

Coffee Chain is modelled on the beer game, developed at MIT in the 1960s. Roasted coffee moves through four stages: a coffee roaster in Bengaluru, a wholesaler in Ahmedabad, a distributor in Vadodara and a café in Vadodara that sells to customers. Each stage holds stock, receives orders from the stage below, sends what it can, and orders from the stage above. Players see only their own stage. Customer demand changes once, early on, by a modest amount, and the swings that follow up the chain are entirely made by the players.

Learning outcomes

  1. Describe the bullwhip effect and recognise it in data.
  2. Explain how delays and local information lead sensible people to over-order.
  3. Explain why counting the supply line (stock already on the way) matters.
  4. Evaluate remedies: information sharing, shorter lead times, steadier ordering.

60-minute lesson plan

0–8Set-up. Show the chain on the start page. Explain the four steps of a week and the costs (₹10 per bag in stock, ₹20 per bag owed, per week). Say the goal is the lowest total cost for the team. Don’t reveal the demand pattern or how many weeks the game lasts.
8–10Teams join. Online: teams of four join with the class code and PIN, and each student gets one stage automatically. Solo: each student plays one stage against the computer.
10–35Play. 24 weeks, about one minute each. No talking between stages about numbers (in a real chain they can’t). Walk round and ask the wholesalers and roasters what they think customers are doing.
35–50The reveal. Before showing the final report, ask each stage to sketch what they think customer demand looked like. Then show the report: demand rose once, from 40 to 80 bags a week. Walk through the orders chart and the stock chart.
50–60Theory. The four classic causes of the bullwhip effect (demand forecast updating, order batching, price fluctuation, rationing and shortage gaming) and remedies such as shared sales data, vendor-managed inventory and shorter lead times.

For a powerful comparison, run two classes (or two halves of a class) as separate online classes: one with classic rules and one with information sharing switched on when you create the class. Compare the teams’ costs on the dashboard.

How it works

  • Each week: goods ordered earlier arrive; an order arrives from the stage below (at the café, from customers); the stage sends as much as it can, and anything it can’t send is owed (backlog) and must be sent later; then the stage orders from the stage above.
  • Delays: an order takes 1 week to reach the stage above. Goods take 2 weeks to travel down each step. The coffee estates always supply the roaster in full, with the same delays.
  • Costs: ₹10 per bag in stock and ₹20 per bag owed, every week, for every stage. The team score is the total for all four stages.
  • Start: each stage has 120 bags in stock, 40 bags arriving in each of the next two weeks, and orders of 40 a week flowing. Everything is in balance.
  • Demand (keep this from students): customers buy 40 bags a week in weeks 1 to 4, then 80 bags a week from week 5 to the end. That is the only change.
  • Length: 24 weeks. The game doesn’t show how many weeks are left, so students don’t run their stock down at the end.
  • Online roles: with 4 members, each runs one stage. With 2 or 3, some members run two stages. With 5 or more, members share stages. The week moves on when all four stages have ordered.
  • Information sharing: an option you choose when creating the class. Every stage then sees the café’s customer sales each week.
  • Solo play: the student runs one stage and the computer runs the other three, ordering the way typical players do.

The final report compares each team with three computer teams playing the same 24 weeks:

Debrief: answer points

1. Why did orders swing so much more at the roaster than at the café?
Each stage sees only the orders from the stage below, not real demand. When orders rise, each stage reads it as a trend, orders extra to rebuild stock and cover the delay, and passes a bigger jump upstream. Delays make each stage over-correct, so the swings grow at every step.
2. When orders to you jumped, what did you guess was happening to demand?
Most players guess that customer demand rose sharply or kept rising. In fact it rose once. Their own orders upstream created the “surge” they reacted to.
3. Did you count the coffee already on the way?
Most players ignore the supply line: they keep ordering while their stock is low, even though plenty is already on its way. When it all arrives, they are flooded. Counting the supply line is the single biggest improvement a player can make.
4. Why does “pass the order on” work so well here?
It adds no extra swing, so every stage sees exactly the real demand change. It isn’t a perfect policy (it lets stock run down), but it shows that over-reacting costs far more than under-reacting.
5. How does sharing customer sales help?
Every stage can plan from real demand instead of guessing from distorted orders. This is the logic behind sharing point-of-sale data, vendor-managed inventory and collaborative planning between partners.
6. Real-life examples
Panic buying (staples and sanitiser in 2020), festive season stock, chip shortages in the car industry, and promotions that cause buy-ahead spikes.

Follow-up work

  1. Draw the chain. Students sketch their own stage’s orders and stock from the final report, then explain each turning point.
  2. Bullwhip ratio. Using the order swing figures, rank the stages and explain the ranking in one paragraph.
  3. Remedy proposal. Recommend two changes to the chain (for example sharing sales data, or cutting shipping time to one week) and predict their effect.

Boundary Bakes (solo)

Students run a bakery stall outside a cricket stadium in Ahmedabad during a 10-day T20 tournament. Each morning they decide how many brownie boxes and fresh sandwiches to make, before they know how many fans will come. Unsold brownies can be sold to a café the next morning for part of their cost; unsold sandwiches are thrown away. This is the classic newsvendor (single-period stock) problem.

Learning outcomes

  1. Explain the trade-off between the cost of having too much and the cost of having too little.
  2. Calculate the critical ratio and use it to choose a stock level above or below the forecast.
  3. Explain why the best service level differs between products.

Lesson plan (about 30 minutes)

0–3Give the class one game code. Students open Boundary Bakes on their own devices.
3–18Students play the 10 days. Don’t mention the critical ratio yet.
18–30Collect results (students paste their copied result line in the class group). Ask who made more than the forecast, and of which product. Then walk through the critical ratio box on the final report.

Each day’s forecast rises as the tournament reaches the final (brownies from 30 to 85, sandwiches from 50 to 70). Actual demand varies around the forecast with a spread of 30%, and the screen tells students the range they can expect on two days out of three. The best amount to make is the forecast plus z spreads, where z comes from the critical ratio: about +0.5 for brownies and −0.8 for sandwiches.

Across 300 test games: Make the forecast averaged ₹61,777 profit, Critical ratio ₹65,789 (better in 93% of games) and Perfect foresight ₹80,702. Making 20% extra of both products, a common “play safe” instinct, does worse than simply making the forecast.

Debrief: answer points

Why make more brownies than the forecast but fewer sandwiches?
A missed brownie sale loses ₹140, but a leftover brownie loses only ₹60, so it pays to over-make. A missed sandwich sale loses only ₹20, but a leftover sandwich loses ₹80, so it pays to under-make and accept some sold-out days.
Isn’t turning customers away bad service?
It is a cost, and the critical ratio weighs it against waste. If goodwill matters, add it to the cost of one too few; the ratio rises and you make more.
What if leftover sandwiches could be sold for ₹40?
One too many would then cost ₹40, the ratio becomes 20 ÷ 60 = 0.33, and the best amount moves up towards the forecast.

Follow-up work

  1. Calculate the critical ratio and best amount for a flower seller, a newspaper vendor or a bakery of the student’s choice.
  2. Explain in one paragraph why a fashion retailer orders less of a risky new style than its forecast.

Season Planner (solo)

Students run production at a garment unit in Surat that makes school uniform sets. Orders build from January to a peak of about 18,000 sets in May, before schools reopen, then fall in June. Each month students set the number of workers, the overtime level and the amount sent to a subcontractor. Stock can be built ahead of the peak. This is aggregate (sales and operations) planning.

Learning outcomes

  1. Compare level, chase and mixed strategies for seasonal demand.
  2. Weigh the costs of hiring, layoffs, overtime, subcontracting, storage and lost sales.
  3. Build and defend a six-month production plan.

Lesson plan (about 40 minutes)

0–5Show the start page and the levers. Give one game code to the class.
5–25Students plan and run the six months. Encourage them to look at the whole forecast before deciding January.
25–40Collect results. Compare a student who used a level plan with one who chased demand. Use the “Where the money went” table to discuss each cost.

How it works

  • Forecast: January 7,000; February 8,000; March 10,000; April 14,000; May 18,000; June 9,000 sets. Actual orders are within 8% of the forecast.
  • Each worker makes 400 sets a month and costs ₹16,000 a month. Hiring costs ₹10,000 per worker (up to 20 a month); a layoff costs ₹15,000.
  • Overtime adds up to 25% of regular output at ₹60 per set. Subcontracting supplies up to 5,000 sets a month at ₹90 per set.
  • Stock costs ₹8 per set per month. Lost orders cost ₹150 per set. Sets left after June are cleared at a loss of ₹50 each.
  • The game starts with 20 workers and 2,000 sets in stock.

Across 200 test games: the Level plan (27 workers all season) averaged ₹31.9 lakh, the Chase plan ₹35.0 lakh and the Best mixed plan ₹30.7 lakh. The best mixed plan was found by the computer searching every combination for the forecast: it builds a modest stock, uses overtime in April and May, and never subcontracts.

Debrief: answer points

Why does chasing demand cost so much here?
The peak is high and short, so a chase plan hires many workers for one or two months and then pays to lay them off. Hiring and layoff costs outweigh the storage it saves.
Why does the best plan use overtime but not subcontracting?
Overtime costs ₹60 a set and subcontracting ₹90, while a regular set costs ₹40 in wages plus storage while it waits. Overtime is a cheap way to add short-term capacity; subcontracting is the most expensive option and is only worth it to avoid lost sales.
What about the people being laid off?
Real companies weigh morale, skills and reputation, which this game doesn’t charge for. That is one reason many firms prefer level or mixed plans even when chase looks cheaper on paper.

Follow-up work

  1. Build the level and chase plans in a spreadsheet and check the game’s costs.
  2. Redo the plan if the layoff cost doubled, or if overtime was capped at 10%.

Export Desk (solo)

Students run the export desk at a pump maker in Rajkot. Eight orders arrive from customers in Hamburg, New York, Dubai and Mombasa, each with a value in euros or US dollars and a delivery deadline. For each order they choose a shipping route, decide whether to lock the exchange rate with a forward contract, and decide whether to insure the cargo. Delays, damage and currency moves are revealed after shipping.

Learning outcomes

  1. Choose a transport mode by weighing freight cost against transit time, deadlines and risk.
  2. Explain exchange-rate risk and how a forward contract removes it, at a small cost.
  3. Explain why exporters insure cargo even though most shipments arrive safely.
  4. Use market news (route disruptions, strikes, price spikes) in logistics decisions.

Lesson plan (about 30 minutes)

0–5Introduce the three decisions and the costs on the start page. Give the class one game code.
5–20Students ship all eight orders.
20–30Collect result lines. Compare the cheapest student with the most cautious one, then use the final report’s strategy table to discuss matching the route to the deadline.

How it works

  • Money: the customer pays in euros (₹92 per € today) or dollars (₹84 per $) when the goods arrive. Making the pumps costs 72% of the order value. Late delivery costs 1% of the order value per day, up to 15%.
  • Routes: to Hamburg, sea via Suez takes 26 days (₹2.0 lakh), via the Cape of Good Hope 36 days (₹2.6 lakh), sea to Dubai then air 12 days (₹5.2 lakh), air 4 days (₹9 lakh). New York is similar but slower and dearer. Dubai and Mombasa have sea or air only.
  • Currency: the rupee drifts up to 3% either way while goods travel, and once per game (on order 2, 3, 5, 6 or 7) the foreign currency jumps: down 7% or up 6% against the rupee. A forward contract locks today’s rate, less 0.6%.
  • Damage: the chance of damage is 7% by sea, 5% by sea-air and 2% by air. Damaged cargo loses 35% of its value. Insurance costs 0.4% of the order value and pays the full loss.
  • Events: one Suez blockage adds 14 days to Suez sailings for one order (orders 3, 4, 6 or 7), usually with a warning. A Hamburg dock strike adds 7 days to one sea shipment to Germany, sometimes with a warning. In 60% of games, one order (4, 6 or 8) comes during an air cargo squeeze that raises air and sea-air prices by 80%, shown before booking.

Across 300 test games: Cheapest every time averaged ₹91.7 lakh profit but ranged from a loss of ₹18 lakh to a profit of ₹120 lakh. Fastest and safest every time averaged ₹72.7 lakh, with almost no variation. Match the route to the deadline (cheapest route expected to arrive with 3 days to spare, rate locked, cargo insured) averaged ₹110 lakh and was best in 89% of games. Students who also react to the shipping news can beat it.

Debrief: answer points

When was air worth it?
Only when a deadline couldn’t be met by sea, as with the short Dubai and Hamburg orders. Late penalties of up to 15% cost far more than the extra freight. When there was plenty of time, air just threw money away.
Why hedge if the rate might move in your favour?
The exporter’s business is making pumps, not betting on currencies. The forward contract costs 0.6% and turns an uncertain amount into a known one, so prices and profits can be planned.
Why insure when damage is rare?
The premium is small (0.4%), but a single uninsured damaged shipment can lose 35% of a large order and wipe out the profit on several others.

Follow-up work

  1. Explain the difference between FOB and CIF Incoterms, and who would have paid the freight and insurance in this game under each.
  2. Compare the landed cost of one order shipped by sea and by air, including the money tied up while the goods travel.

Go Global (solo)

Students lead international expansion for Sona Herbals, an Ayurvedic skincare brand from Ahmedabad, with ₹10 crore to invest. Over six rounds (three years in half-year steps) they choose how to be present in five markets: the UAE, the UK, the USA, Germany and Kenya. In each market they can be absent or use an online store, a distributor, a joint venture or their own subsidiary. World news arrives after each round’s decisions.

Learning outcomes

  1. Compare entry modes on cost, control, risk and profit share.
  2. Explain the case for entering step by step: learning about a market before committing.
  3. Weigh market size and growth against distance and country risk.
  4. Explain how local production protects against tariffs, and how fixed costs raise risk in a downturn.

Lesson plan (about 40 minutes)

0–8Walk through the entry-mode table on the start page: who reaches how many buyers, who keeps how much of the margin, and what each costs. Give one game code.
8–28Students play the six rounds.
28–40Collect results. Ask the highest and lowest scorers to describe their path in each market, then compare “All in from day one” with “Step by step”.

How it works

  • Markets (market size per half-year, growth a year): UAE ₹5 crore, 12%; UK ₹7 crore, 6%; USA ₹16 crore, 8%; Germany ₹9 crore, 4%; Kenya ₹3 crore, 24%. Set-up costs rise with distance (USA 1.6×, Germany 1.4×, Kenya 1.3×, UK 1.2×).
  • Entry modes: online store (reaches 20% of buyers, keeps 32% of sales, ₹0.2 crore set-up, ₹0.1 crore a round); distributor (50%, 15%, ₹0.3 crore); joint venture (70%, 18% after a 50:50 split, ₹1.2 crore, ₹0.3 crore a round); own subsidiary (90%, 36%, ₹2.5 crore, ₹0.7 crore a round). New operations ramp up over two or three rounds. Leaving a joint venture or subsidiary costs money.
  • Learning: a joint venture or subsidiary reaches 40% of its normal buyers with no experience in the market, 70% after one round there, and 100% after two. Opened with no experience, there is a 50% chance the product needs reworking (₹1 crore, and sales down 60% for two rounds); with experience, 10%.
  • World news (same for everyone with the same code): Kenyan shilling falls 25%; a 20% US tariff hits all modes except a subsidiary, which produces locally; German rules stop imported products for one round; a joint-venture partner dispute in one market; the pound falls 10%; a spending slump cuts US sales by 35% for two rounds; and a viral boost lifts sales by 30% in one market.
  • Cash and score: changes are paid up front and can’t exceed cash. If cash falls below zero, interest is charged at 12% a round. Company worth = cash at the end plus two years of profit at the final rate.

Across 300 test games: Distributors everywhere averaged ₹37.2 crore, All in from day one ₹57.6 crore (ranging from ₹47.6 to ₹71.2 crore) and Step by step ₹60.4 crore, best in 89% of games. All in wins only when its gamble on untested markets pays off.

Debrief: answer points

Why not open subsidiaries everywhere straight away?
They are expensive, take time to ramp up and often misread an unfamiliar market. A cheap first step (distributor or online) builds knowledge, shows which markets are worth it, and cuts the risk of a costly mistake. This is the logic of gradual internationalisation.
When is a joint venture better than a subsidiary?
When the market is smaller or less familiar: the partner brings local knowledge, and set-up costs are lower. The price is sharing profit and the risk of partner disputes.
How did local production help?
A subsidiary avoided the US tariff and the German import rule. Its higher fixed costs, however, hurt more in the US spending slump.

Follow-up work

  1. Rank the five markets for a real Indian brand of the student’s choice using size, growth, distance and risk.
  2. Write a one-page entry plan for one market: which mode first, what would make you commit more, and when.

Factory Footprint (solo)

Students run operations at Kiran Ceramics, a tile maker in Morbi with six plants, selling in India, the Gulf, Europe and the USA. Over six years they decide where to build or close plants: in Morbi, Ras Al Khaimah (UAE), Poland or Monterrey (Mexico). Each year the plants automatically send their tiles to the markets where they earn the most. World news arrives after each year’s decisions.

Learning outcomes

  1. Calculate landed cost: cost to make, plus freight, plus duty, plus other fees.
  2. Explain why the cheapest place to make something is not always the cheapest place to supply a market from.
  3. Show how tariffs, carbon border fees and freight costs shift the best location for production.
  4. Weigh the lead time and fixed cost of a new plant against capacity shortages and concentration risk.

Lesson plan (about 40 minutes)

0–8Open the game together and read the landed cost table on the first year’s screen: why does the cheapest source differ by market? Give one game code.
8–28Students play the six years.
28–40Collect results. Compare the students who stayed in India with those who built abroad, then use the final report to discuss timing: who built before the duty and the carbon fee arrived, and who reacted afterwards?

How it works

  • Markets (demand in year 1, growth a year, price per box): India 70 lakh boxes, 7%, ₹420; Gulf 20 lakh, 5%, ₹560; Europe 30 lakh, 2%, ₹700; USA 35 lakh, 4%, ₹760. Actual demand varies up to 5% from the forecast.
  • Sites (cost to make a box, cost to build a plant, yearly site cost): Morbi ₹260, ₹60 crore, none; UAE ₹300, ₹80 crore, ₹8 crore; Poland ₹360, ₹110 crore, ₹6 crore; Mexico ₹400, ₹90 crore, ₹10 crore. Every plant makes up to 25 lakh boxes a year and costs ₹6 crore a year to run. New plants produce from the following year. Closing a plant costs ₹15 crore.
  • Freight runs from ₹15–35 a box within a region to ₹170–180 a box across the world. Import duty is 20% into India and 5% into the other markets, charged on the cost of the goods plus freight. Plants in the UAE, Poland and Mexico pay no duty into their own region.
  • World news (the same for everyone with the same code): the USA adds a 30% duty on tiles from India (from year 2, 3 or 4); Europe charges a carbon border fee of ₹100 a box on tiles from outside Europe (from year 3, 4 or 5); freight rates double for one year; a gas cut leaves one site with a quarter of its capacity for one year; and a building boom raises demand by 20% in India or 50% in the Gulf.
  • Score: company worth = cash at the end plus two years of profit at the final year’s rate. Starting cash is ₹150 crore, and plant changes can’t cost more than the cash in hand.

Across 300 test games: Stay in India averaged ₹2,163 crore, A plant in every region ₹2,438 crore, and Plants where landed cost gaps are biggest (Mexico first, then a second Mexican plant and one in Poland) ₹2,507 crore, best in 93% of games. A plant in every region wins only when a gas cut hits Mexico in the final year.

Debrief: answer points

Why build in Mexico when making tiles there costs ₹140 a box more?
Landed cost, not production cost, decides. Mexico saves ₹135 a box in freight to the USA and pays no duty, and after the 30% US duty on Indian tiles the gap is large. The Indian plants are then free to serve India’s growing market.
Why does timing matter so much?
A plant takes a year to build. Students who waited for the duty or carbon fee to arrive paid it for at least a year before their new plant could help. Building ahead is a bet; waiting is a cost.
What does the gas cut teach?
A network with all its plants in one place has no backup. When the cut hits Morbi, a single-site company loses most of a year’s sales, while a spread-out network keeps shipping.

Follow-up work

  1. Pick a real Indian product that is exported. Build a landed cost table for two possible production sites serving one export market.
  2. Explain what a carbon border fee such as the EU’s CBAM means for Indian exporters of steel, aluminium or cement.

Freight Buyer (solo)

Students buy container shipping for Kaveri Home Textiles, a towel and bed-linen exporter in Tiruppur that ships about 40 containers a quarter from Chennai to Rotterdam. Before each of eight quarters they book space on contract with Anchor Line (large and reliable) and Seabird Line (smaller and cheaper). Anything not covered goes on the spot market at that quarter’s rate, which is revealed afterwards.

Learning outcomes

  1. Explain the difference between contract and spot freight, and when each is cheaper.
  2. Explain why a carrier’s reliability matters more than its rate when capacity is short.
  3. Balance the risk of booking too much (paying for empty slots) against booking too little (paying spot rates or waiting).
  4. Use market signals, while knowing they can be wrong.

Lesson plan (about 30 minutes)

0–5Explain contract and spot freight using the start page. Give one game code.
5–20Students play the eight quarters.
20–30Collect results. Compare students who relied on the spot market with those who booked contracts, then look at the rate chart on the final report together.

How it works

  • Need: the forecast is 36, 40, 46 and 42 containers in the four quarters of year 1, and 5% more in year 2. The real number can be up to 15% higher or lower.
  • Rates: a normal spot rate is about ₹1.6 lakh a container. Anchor Line’s contract rate is half last quarter’s spot rate plus half of ₹1.92 lakh, so it follows the market slowly. Seabird Line charges 7% less.
  • Market states: normal (spot below 1.5 times normal), tight (1.5 to 2.2 times), and ships full (above 2.2 times). Anchor Line keeps 100%, 100% and 85% of your booking in these states; Seabird Line keeps 100%, 50% and 30%. Spot space is unlimited in a normal market, 40% of the quarter’s need when tight, and 10% when ships are full.
  • Costs: unused contract space costs 50% of its rate. Containers that can’t ship wait for the next quarter and cost ₹2 lakh each for every quarter they wait. Any still waiting at the end ship at the last spot rate plus one more delay charge.
  • The market (the same for everyone with the same code): a squeeze starts in quarter 3, 4 or 5. In half the games it is severe (spot rates about triple, then fall below normal); in a quarter it is moderate (up to 1.8 times); in a quarter it is mild (up to 1.3 times). In 70% of games, shipping news warns one quarter ahead; some games also have a false alarm.
  • Score: total cost over the eight quarters. Lower is better.

Across 300 test games: Spot only averaged ₹803 lakh (from ₹510 to ₹1,113 lakh), Cheapest contract (everything with Seabird Line) ₹727 lakh, and Reliable base, spot top-up (80% of the forecast with Anchor Line) ₹693 lakh, with the smallest range. The reliable base was cheapest in 53% of games, including every severe squeeze; spot only was cheapest in 27% (mild markets) and the cheapest contract in 20%.

Debrief: answer points

Why not buy everything on the spot market?
In calm times it is cheapest. But when ships fill up, spot rates triple and space disappears, so containers wait and customers are let down. One bad year can cost more than several calm years save.
Why did the cheaper line cost more in the end?
When space was scarce, Seabird Line cancelled most bookings to sell the space to customers paying more. The cancelled containers had to go on an expensive, crowded spot market or wait. A contract is only worth as much as the carrier’s willingness to keep it.
Why book about 80% of the forecast rather than all of it?
Need varies by up to 15%. Booking a reliable base below the forecast avoids paying for empty slots, and the spot market tops up the rest when it is cheap.

Follow-up work

  1. Look up how container rates from Asia to Europe moved in 2020–2022 and explain what happened to shippers who relied on the spot market.
  2. Write a one-page freight buying policy for an exporter: what share on contract, with which kind of carrier, and when to change it.
